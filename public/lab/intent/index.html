Prototype · BCI cursor control

Intent Lab

Steer a cursor through a simulated brain–computer interface decoder. Your hand plays the brain. A Web Worker plays the decoder: it bins your movement every few milliseconds and adds noise, drift, delay and dropped packets. The page has to turn that stream into a cursor you can aim with.

Tune the filter and the spring, then run the timed grid task. The page measures bits per second and the time from your input to the drawn frame, against a 15 ms budget.

What to try

      
    
        
  1. Raw against smoothed. Do one run on “Raw decoder” and one on “Smoothed”. Dwell is where jitter hurts most, because the cursor has to hold still on a cell.
    2. 
        
  2. Spring stiffness. A stiffer spring feels faster, and below ζ ≈ 0.7 it overshoots. Compare the median time to select at k = 200 and k = 800.
    3. 
        
  3. Delay. Raise the delay to 6 bins. Reaches start to overshoot, because you correct for where the cursor was, not where it is.
    4. 
        
  4. The Midas touch. After a dwell selection, that cell is disarmed until the cursor leaves it. Resting on a cell never selects it twice.

What the numbers mean

      

        
Bitrate

        
log₂(N − 1) × max(correct − missed, 0) ÷ seconds, for N cells. Missed selections cancel correct ones, so guessing doesn’t pay.

        
Stream → frame

        
From the moment the worker posts a decoded bin to the moment the frame that uses it is drawn. This is the page’s own share of the budget. On a 60 Hz display, waiting for the next frame alone can take up to 16.7 ms.

        
Intent → frame

        
From your input event to the drawn frame. It includes the bin wait and the simulated delay you set.

        
Limits

        
“Drawn” means submitted to the browser, not lit on the display, and browsers coarsen their timers. The filter and spring add lag that timestamps can’t show, so compare median time to select as well.
